You Pay for 50kg. They Give You 45.

By Simon Erhiaworon November 14, 2026 6 min read Market Story

Last week, at 6:45 in the morning, inside a gas sellers' WhatsApp group in my town, one man sent a single message:

"The last products I bought from them, I was short 4 to 5 kg on each. And they don't even have a scale."

Another man replied: "Wow."

Just "Wow." Because what else is there to say? Everybody in that group has seen it before. It has become normal. And that is the problem.

The theft that became tradition

If you sell LPG in Nigeria, you know this dance. You take your cylinder to the gas plant. You pay for 50kg. They fill it, they load it, you drive back to your shop. But the cylinder does not carry 50kg. It carries 45. Sometimes 46, if luck is on your side.

You cannot check. The plant does not even have a scale. The only scale in the whole equation is the one you use to sell to your customer — and it is you the customer will blame when the gas finishes too quickly.

So the shortage travels in a full circle: the plant shorts the seller, the seller is blamed by the buyer. Nobody robs anyone at gunpoint. The system just steals a little, every day, from everybody — and everybody has accepted it as normal.

This same week, I watched sellers in that group compare prices from different plants like it was the stock market — 1,400 here, 1,500 there, "price changes every day." One of them wrote: "God abeg oo." Another answered: "Use your brain and stop complaining."

That is the mood of the market right now. Prices climb. Weights fall. And the people holding the whole country's cooking gas supply — the small roadside seller — absorb the pain from both sides.

The second thing nobody tells sellers

Now let me tell you the part that shocks people.

You know that thing some sellers do — connecting one cylinder to another with a hose, pumping gas from one into the other, so one cylinder can serve as a shop? The government has a name for it: decanting. And it is illegal. The regulator, NMDPRA, enforces against it — and it is dangerous. It is how cylinders explode. It is how fire takes innocent shops and houses.

But hear me clearly: the seller is not the villain of this story. Decanting does not happen because sellers love crime. It happens because doing things the right way costs money a roadside seller does not have.

And that is exactly why the Federal Government created the National Cylinder Recirculation Programme (NCRP). The plan is simple: kill the hose, and make everybody swap cylinders like rechargeable batteries. You bring your empty, certified cylinder; you collect a full one with a QR tag, already weighed and recorded in the government system. No hose. No shortage. No argument.

That future is coming. Whether it reaches Ughelli next year or in five years, it is coming. The only question is: when it arrives, which sellers will be standing on the right side of it?
